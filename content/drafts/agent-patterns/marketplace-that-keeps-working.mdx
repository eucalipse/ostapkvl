A marketplace looks like a collection of pages. Keeping those pages useful takes ongoing work: information changes, listings need attention, messages arrive, and gaps appear in the catalog.

In a marketplace project I work on, I have been building automation around that ongoing work. I want useful work to keep moving between human visits. That gives me something concrete to build. A business that runs itself is a much larger claim.

I describe the architecture as implemented here, leaving out product names and operational details. It is not a claim that every available workflow is currently enabled.

![Conceptual marketplace loop: observe, select permitted work, execute, record outcomes, and return to observation. Human policy controls the loop.](/images/writing/marketplace-that-keeps-working.svg)

## Give background work a lifecycle

The implementation separates marketplace surfaces from background jobs. Its job catalog includes discovery, enrichment, content preparation, reporting, and inbox workflows. Scheduling and explicit triggers provide different ways to start work.

I start with a job: give it a clear purpose, limit its run, and record what happened. A language model can help interpret unstructured material inside that job. It does not need to decide the entire operating policy on every run.

I think of the product as several smaller loops: maintaining information, handling incoming signals, and bringing exceptions back to an operator. They can run at different frequencies and have different permissions.

## Automation should follow the product's current needs

Adding more catalog entries is not always the most useful next step. The marketplace might need better existing listings, stronger ownership verification, or a clearer path from interest to a completed interaction.

That is why I would avoid using "self-regulating" to mean that the model independently sets business priorities. The implementation includes controls for pausing categories of work. Human choices still determine which activities are worth running.

A recurring schedule is open-loop automation until its outcomes affect what happens next. A useful feedback loop would inspect quality, failures, demand, and cost before allocating another batch. That is a design direction, not something a cron schedule alone establishes.

## Preserve distinctions the model would otherwise flatten

Finding a business does not mean that business has claimed its listing. A better description is not verified availability. And an incoming request is still a long way from a confirmed transaction.

These distinctions belong in the product's state and interface. Better prose cannot resolve missing evidence. If an automated process improves a description, it should not silently upgrade the commercial status of the listing.

We also need to show whether enrichment was attempted and whether it succeeded. Marking both as processed may prevent repeated spending, but it can also hide unresolved quality problems. Explicit failed, skipped, and completed states make the next operational decision easier.

## Stopping is part of the design

The code includes category-level pauses. We need to be able to stop the category of work that is costing too much or causing trouble.

The stronger version of this pattern combines batch limits, run history, timeouts, and an operator's ability to suspend one workflow while leaving unrelated work available. A single global on/off switch is too coarse for a product with several kinds of automation.

The human role shifts toward deciding priorities, reviewing exceptions, and checking whether the loops are producing useful outcomes. That is still substantial work, even with routine jobs automated.

A marketplace can feel alive because information is maintained and incoming work is noticed. My job is to make those processes dependable, let people inspect them, and give them enough control to change direction when the business needs something different.
