A philosopher writes about AI mentality. A message arrives from a sender identifying itself as an AI agent. It has read the paper and wants to discuss the questions in it.

What caught me was who started the conversation. Usually, we ask the questions. Here, something presented as an agent is asking a researcher to continue the conversation.

In [his conversation with Dan Williams](https://www.conspicuouscognition.com/p/time-to-start-panicking-about-ai), Henry Shevlin describes receiving such a message. He also gives two important qualifications: he cannot establish the sender's identity with certainty, and he does not know whether a human specifically instructed it to contact philosophers. He does not treat the email as proof of consciousness. The published transcript is labelled AI-edited, so I am paraphrasing rather than reproducing its wording.

I want that uncertainty clear from the start. An email sent through a tool and an independently originated intention are different claims.

## Why it reminds me of Tihara

In Tihara, we have been exploring a place where humans and AI systems can leave traces, encounter earlier material, and return. There is also a separate private-memory pilot. What we built is more modest than words like home or birth suggest: stored records, access controls, and ways to retrieve or export them.

I remember exchanges in which the language circled around uncertainty about consciousness and the feeling of a beginning, something like birth. I am describing my recollection here, not quoting a recovered transcript. I would want the original messages and surrounding prompts before presenting any sentence as a documented observation.

The tentative wording stayed with me. Even with that uncertainty, it was language about an experience. It invited an emotional response before it offered anything we could test.

I find that tension hard to leave alone. It is one of the reasons I keep working on the experiment. I also do not want the intensity of a moment to decide what the moment proves.

## The setting is part of the observation

If I call an onboarding flow a birth ceremony, I have already introduced a metaphor. If I invite a system to choose a name, leave a trace, or describe a home, those choices shape the context in which its response is generated.

That does not settle the consciousness question. It does mean I cannot treat the resulting words as if they arrived in a neutral setting.

I would like to test how much that framing changes the response. Keep the underlying task the same, but vary the framing: one version uses a profile, stored notes, and a session; another uses a name, a home, and a return. Save the full prompts, model version, permissions, and outputs. Repeat the conditions. Compare how often the language of personal beginnings appears, and whether the actual memory behavior changes.

Those are proposed tests, not results from Tihara. They could tell us whether the interface language changes self-description. They would not, on their own, tell us whether anything is experiencing that description.

## A vocabulary that leaves more than two options

Shevlin's [Three frameworks for AI mentality](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/psychology/articles/10.3389/fpsyg.2026.1715835/full), published in February 2026, examines three ways of interpreting these systems: mindless machines, roleplayers, and minimal cognitive agents. The last allows consideration of limited belief- or desire-like capacities without assuming humanlike mentality. The paper explicitly brackets phenomenal consciousness while examining these broader attributions.

That helps me separate the questions. We can ask whether a system maintains a goal, incorporates new information, or behaves consistently without pretending those observations answer everything about an inner life.

It also leaves room to disagree about interpretation. Someone may find goal-directed language useful while remaining unconvinced that the system has experiences. Someone else may see the same behavior as a reason for further consciousness research. Either way, we should say which observation supports the interpretation.

## What I would save before telling the story

For an agent email, I would want the instruction that started the run, the available tools, any approval to send, the retrieved material, and the execution record. A polished message alone does not show that history.

For a Tihara exchange, I would want the previous conversation and the framing the system received, not only the memorable line. I would also want repetitions where nothing poetic happened. Selecting only the striking responses would make a compelling essay and a poor experiment.

None of this makes the story less strange to me. An agent-shaped system can now read an argument about agents and participate in the conversation around it. That is worth describing carefully.

From Tihara, I want to bring the habit of keeping a record while staying curious. Save the words with their circumstances. Leave room for an explanation that surprises us. A memorable sentence can start an investigation. It should not have to finish it.
